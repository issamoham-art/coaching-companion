Ground rules for the practice work

  1. Use real people from your own team wherever the instruction allows. Generic answers produce generic skill.
  2. Write before you speak. Writing a coaching question forces a precision that talking does not.
  3. In role-plays, play the representative honestly — including the resistance. A compliant role-play teaches nothing.
  4. Every exercise has a debrief. The debrief is where the learning lands, not the exercise itself.

Course objective

To improve representative performance by using coaching and counseling as an everyday process.

Not a technique for the annual review. What happens in the car between calls, in the five minutes after a difficult visit, and in the Monday phone call about a stalled account.

The field manager's structural problem

A first-line manager in pharmaceutical sales sees a fraction of actual performance. Representatives spend 80–90% of their working time alone. You get a handful of joint calls per cycle, numbers that arrive weeks after the behaviour that produced them, and a CRM record reflecting what the rep chose to enter.

You cannot supervise your way to performance in that structure. You can only develop your way there.

Module 1

Foundations of Performance Dialogue

Objectives: define coaching and distinguish it from direction; explain the cost of a directive default; diagnose whether a situation calls for coaching or counseling.

What is coaching?

Coaching is a method of improving performance by helping an individual discover and unlock their own potential, using questions rather than instructions.

Training improves skill by telling. Coaching operates on the premise that the representative frequently already holds the answer, or enough of it, and that your job is to draw it out rather than supply it.

Consider the difference between a tour guide and a mountain guide. The tour guide tells you where to look and what to think; you see a great deal and remember little. The mountain guide asks about your fitness, your objective and your fears, then helps you choose the route; you arrive tired, capable, and able to climb the next one without them.

Coaching is asking. Feedback is telling.

Coaching — askingFeedback — telling
"What's your read on the situation?"
"What options have you considered?"
"What would success look like with this account?"
"What's standing in your way?"		 "In the call with Dr. Rania you opened with a question about her patient population before mentioning the product. She talked for four minutes before you presented anything."

"When she raised price, you moved straight to the access programme without asking what was behind the objection."

Coaching builds the representative's capacity for self-correction. Feedback supplies the raw data that self-correction needs. You need both, and you need to know which one you are doing at any given moment.

The cost of directing

What directing costsWhat coaching returns
Dependency — you supply answers, reps stop generating them. Your phone rings more, not less.Ideas surface from the person closest to the customer.
Reduced motivation — autonomy is removed.Ownership of the solution, and therefore commitment to it.
Lost development — a difficult account becomes a transaction instead of a lesson.Development happens inside normal work.
Weaker commitment — people comply with plans handed to them.Confidence and independence grow with each solved problem.

The honest trade-off: coaching takes longer per conversation and less time in aggregate. If your calendar is consumed by problems your reps bring you, you are paying the directing tax.

When to coach and when to counsel

The diagnostic is not the size of the problem. It is the representative's awareness and cooperation.

Use COACHING whenUse COUNSELING when
Aware — they recognise the gap.
Cooperative — willing to discuss and explore.

Example: a rep builds excellent physician relationships but rarely closes for commitment. She knows it. She wants to fix it.		 Unaware — they do not see the behaviour as a problem.
Unconcerned — they see it and do not care.

Example: a rep is repeatedly late to appointments. "The doctors keep me waiting anyway." Coaching questions here produce evasion, not insight.

CS 1.1Ahmed and Hany: The Same Problem, Two Conversations20 min
Purpose: see the mechanical difference between telling and coachingFormat: read individually, discuss in table groups

Background. Ahmed is a district sales manager who has always prided himself on being decisive. Hany is a capable rep whose numbers on a newly launched product are behind target.

Version A — The Telling Approach

Ahmed: "Hany, your numbers on the new launch are down. Here's what you need to do. I've written a new call script — lead with the cost-per-patient data, then go to the safety profile. Use it with every physician this cycle. Also increase your call rate to ten a day. Report back Friday."

Hany: "Okay."

Result. Hany follows the instructions. Numbers improve marginally for two weeks and then flatten. He feels micromanaged, has learned nothing transferable, and the next time an account stalls he waits for Ahmed.

Version B — The Coaching Approach

Ahmed: "Hany, the new product numbers are behind our goal. Let's talk about it. What's your read from the field?"

Hany: "Doctors seem resistant. They say they're happy with what they're already prescribing."

Ahmed: "That's useful. When you say 'resistant' — what specifically are they saying or doing? Walk me through the last conversation."

(Hany describes a call with Dr. Rania — she listened, said the current therapy was working, and moved on.)

Ahmed: "Thank you. Based on what you just described, what do you think her actual objection was?"

Hany: "Hmm. I've been leading with cost savings. But she never mentioned cost. She talked about her patients being stable. Maybe she's worried about switching risk, not price."

Ahmed: "That sounds like a hypothesis worth testing. How would you test it?"

Hany: "I could open the next call by asking which of her patients she'd consider not stable — the ones with tolerability issues. Then the side-effect data becomes relevant instead of the price."

Ahmed: "What would you need to make that work?"

Hany: "The tolerability sub-analysis. And I should practise the opening question — it needs to not sound like a trap."

Result. Hany diagnosed the problem, generated the approach, identified his own resource gap and owns the plan. He will apply the same logic to the next stalled account without Ahmed present.

Module 2

The Core Coaching Process

Objectives: apply a four-part framework to a field performance conversation; separate observable behaviour from judgement; write a behavioural standard.

The four coaching questions

  1. What are the results? — observation and data
  2. What caused the results? — analysis and impact
  3. What are the standards? — clarifying expectations
  4. What do we do about it? — action planning

Skipping step one produces argument. Skipping step three produces a conversation about opinion. Skipping step four produces a pleasant chat.

"I am willing to analyse my actions." → "I participate fully." → "I make judgements about my own results." → "I can see the need to change and I am part of the solution."

This matters more in pharmaceutical field sales than almost anywhere else, precisely because the representative works unobserved for the overwhelming majority of their time. Self-diagnosis is not a nice-to-have — it is the only mechanism that operates when you are not there.

Behaviour versus judgement

A behaviour has two properties: you can see or hear it, and two independent observers would agree on what happened. A judgement has neither — it is a conclusion drawn from behaviour and then presented as if it were the behaviour.

Judgement (ineffective)Behaviour (effective)
"You were unprofessional in the clinic visit.""You arrived ten minutes after the agreed time and the physician had moved on to her next patient."
"You have a bad attitude.""In the last three cycle meetings, when a new campaign was introduced, you said 'this won't work here' before any details were presented."
"Your presentation was weak.""The presentation did not include the Q2 territory data, and when the regional manager asked for it you said you would send it later."
"You're not a team player.""Yesterday when Sara asked for help covering the hospital account, you said you were too busy and left at 4pm."
"You need to be more customer-focused.""In the call with Dr. Rania you spoke for six of the eight minutes and asked one question."
Open with a judgement and the representative's attention shifts entirely to defending their identity. Nothing after that is processed. Open with behaviour and the conversation stays about an action — which a person can change without conceding they are a bad person.

Analysis and impact

The critical difference between a traditional manager and a coach is who does the analysing. The traditional manager analyses, judges and directs. The coach guides the representative to analyse their own behaviour and judge its consequence.

Manager: "Let's talk about the call with Dr. Sarah. Tell me what you did when you opened."

Rep: "I asked an open question about her experience with patients who have condition X."

Manager: "And what happened after you asked that?" (impact)

Rep: "She opened up. She told a story about a difficult case and her frustration with the current options."

Manager: "What was the effect of that on the rest of the call?" (rep judges the impact)

Rep: "It gave me exactly the opening I needed. I could position the tolerability data against a problem she'd just described herself, instead of me asserting it."

Manager: "That's right. What does that tell you about how you want to open with the other specialists on your list?"

Note what the manager did not say: "Good job asking an open-ended question." The rep reached the conclusion, which means the rep owns it, which means the rep will do it again on Tuesday without supervision.

Setting standards

One of the primary reasons for poor performance is that representatives do not know what is expected of them.

A behavioural standard has three components: action or words, conditions, measure.

Vague expectationClear behavioural standard
"Be prepared for your calls.""For every scheduled call with a top-tier physician, you will bring a written call objective, one supporting clinical reference, and one planned closing question."
"Follow up on leads quickly.""All inbound requests from the medical information portal receive a first response within one working day, logged in CRM."
"Manage your territory effectively.""You will visit each A-list account at least twice per cycle, with a documented call outcome for each visit."
"Improve your product knowledge.""By the end of this cycle you will present the primary endpoint, the two main secondary endpoints, and the discontinuation rate from the pivotal trial without notes."

Module 3

Mastering Essential Coaching Skills

Objectives: elicit and improve ideas without taking them over; identify and move up the listening levels; deliver behavioural feedback; use the Johari Window.

Eliciting and modifying ideas

TechniqueIn practice
ExplainSay why their ideas are needed. "You were in the room with Dr. Karim. I wasn't. Your read is better than mine."
QuestionOpen-ended only. Avoid anything answerable with yes or no.
DemonstrateVisible active listening. Put the phone away. Turn the car radio off.
ReinforceA nod, sustained attention, and silence long enough for them to keep going.
The most underused eliciting technique is silence. Most managers wait about two seconds after asking a question before filling the gap. Wait eight.

Modifying an imperfect idea

  • Have the rep evaluate it. "What could go wrong with that? What would need to be true for it to work?"
  • Support the parts with merit. "The timing is right — going in before the formulary review is smart. Say more about how you'd handle the pharmacist."
  • Don't let imperfect ideas be lost. Analysing a weak idea builds the rep's judgement. Killing it builds their silence.

Three levels of listening

Level 1 — Internal

Focus is on you. You hear the words while your mind constructs your reply or relates it to your own experience. Most management conversations happen here.

Level 2 — Focused

Sharp focus on the other person. Words, tone, emphasis. You are not preparing your response.

Level 3 — Global

You hear what is said and what is not. Energy, hesitation, what they avoided, what they said quickly to get past.

Techniques: paraphrasing ("So if I'm hearing you right, the issue isn't access, it's that once you're in the room you're not sure what to lead with"); summarising; reflecting feelings ("It sounds like that call left you pretty discouraged"); clarifying questions ("When you say the account is 'difficult', what specifically happens?").

Feedback

Feedback is advice given in the present, addressing past behaviour, to influence future performance.
MisconceptionTruth
"We do annual appraisals, that's enough."Waiting twelve months means the rep repeats the behaviour ~200 working days before hearing about it. Feedback has a short half-life.
"It's not my job."It is specifically the first-line manager's job — you are the only person who sees the rep in front of the customer.
"No news is good news."No news is a void, and people fill voids with their own assumptions. You also never identify what is working well enough to spread.
"Too much praise spoils people."Praise grounded in specific behaviour develops people. Vague praise carries no information and stops meaning anything.
"I gave feedback — I told Samy about my problem with Ahmed."That is talking about a colleague to a third party. Feedback goes directly to the person concerned.
Weak: "I'm glad you're on my team."
Strong: "Your approach to the distributor stock issue — mapping their delivery cycle against our order dates — is why we didn't lose two weeks of supply. That's the kind of thinking I want the rest of the team to see."

Format: groups of three — Speaker, Listener, Observer. Rotate.Time: 3 rounds × 8 min + 6 min debrief

Setup. The Speaker talks for three minutes about a genuine current challenge with an account or a team member. No hypotheticals.

  • Minute 0–1 — Listener at Level 1. Deliberately listen internally: plan your response, glance away, relate it to your own experience out loud. This will feel unpleasant. That is the point.
  • Minute 1–3 — Listener at Level 2/3. Full attention, no advice. Only paraphrasing, reflecting and clarifying questions. No solutions permitted.
  • Minute 3–5 — Feedback. Speaker describes what the switch felt like. Observer reports specific behaviours that changed.

AC 3.2Blind Spot Exchange20 min
Purpose: use the Johari model live; experience receiving feedbackFormat: pairs who have observed each other during the program

Round 1 — Disclosure (5 min). Each person shares one thing about their own management style the other could not have known — a fear, a habit they dislike in themselves, a situation they find hard. (Shrinking the façade.)

Facilitator note. Round 2 is where the group learns that receiving feedback is a skill, not a passive event. If the room fills with explanations and defence, stop and name it — that is the most valuable teaching moment in the module.

Module 5

Navigating Difficult Conversations: Counseling

Objectives: distinguish workplace counseling from coaching and from therapy; work the five-step escalation and return to coaching at the point of recognition; diagnose why performance is not improving.

A boundary first. As a manager you are not a therapist. Workplace counseling does not address personal or psychological issues. If personal problems surface, your role is to refer, not to treat.
Counseling is the process used by a manager when the representative is unaware of, or unconcerned about, the consequences of their behaviour.

The representative's internal position differs sharply from a coaching situation: "My results are fine, so there's no problem." Genuine unawareness of the effect of their actions. "What I do doesn't affect anyone else." Reluctance to examine their own behaviour at all.

The escalation principle. Each step is more serious than the last. At any step, the moment the representative genuinely recognises the problem, you stop escalating and switch back to coaching. This is not a punishment sequence to be completed — it is a ladder you climb only as far as necessary and descend as soon as you can.

The five steps

StepWhat you sayIf they recognise it
1. Focus on the behaviour"Karim, in the last four cycle meetings you have arrived between ten and fifteen minutes after the start time."Stop. Go to coaching.
2. Impact on others"When you arrive late, we either wait — which costs seven people ten minutes — or we repeat the opening, which means the meeting runs over and everyone's field time is cut. Last cycle that happened four times."Stop. Go to coaching.
3. Consequences to them"Punctuality and respect for colleagues' time are part of how reliability is assessed here. You've told me you want the key account role. This behaviour is now part of how you're perceived, and it works against that."Stop. Go to coaching.
4. Manager's alternatives"I require you to be on time for all future cycle meetings. I am documenting this as a formal verbal warning. If the behaviour continues, the next step is a written warning."Formal step. Documented.
5. Ending the relationshipConducted with HR, fully documented, and never a surprise to the representative.—
The discipline. Do not skip steps to save time, and do not climb further than the response requires. Managers fail this in both directions — some jump to Step 4 out of frustration, others cycle at Step 1 for a year and then escalate to Step 4 without warning.

Debrief

Troubleshooting: why isn't performance improving?

Before repeating the same process louder, diagnose:

  • Factors other than the observed behaviour. A personal issue, health, a change in circumstance. Is the behaviour a symptom of something you have not asked about?
  • The deviation is unimportant. Are you coaching call rate while the actual problem is targeting?
  • Consequences are not real. Have consequences been stated and observed to occur?
  • You are doing most of the talking. If sessions are 90% you, they are not coaching sessions.
  • Past experience says you will not act. A history of stating consequences without follow-through empties your words regardless of construction. The hardest to fix; takes months.
  • You are reinforcing the wrong behaviour. The rep who escalates every problem gets more of your attention than the one who solves them quietly.
  • The standard was never actually agreed. Was it stated, or assumed? Could the rep repeat it back?

The principles, compressed

  • Ask, don't tell.
  • Focus on behaviour, not judgement.
  • Listen more than you speak. Target 30/70.
  • Foster ownership, because ownership produces commitment.
  • Adapt your style to the person and the task, not to your own comfort.
  • Diagnose awareness and cooperation before choosing coaching or counseling.
  • Escalate only as far as required, and return to coaching the moment recognition appears.

Appendix

Coaching Question Bank

Opening / Contract

  • What would make this a good use of the next twenty minutes?
  • What do you want to leave this conversation with?
  • Where would you like to start?

Understanding

  • What's your read on what's happening?
  • Walk me through the last conversation — what did you say, what did they say?
  • What have you already tried?
  • What's different about this account compared with the ones that are working?

Surfacing the real issue

  • When you say "difficult", what specifically happens?
  • What do you think is actually behind that objection?
  • What's the single biggest obstacle?
  • What are you assuming that might not be true?

Generating options

  • What options have you considered?
  • If anything were possible, what would you do?
  • What would you advise a colleague in this position?
  • What's the option you've dismissed — and why?

Testing an idea

  • What would need to be true for that to work?
  • What could go wrong with it?
  • What's the strongest argument against your own plan?

Commitment & close

  • What is the very first step?
  • What do you need from me?
  • By when will you have started?
  • On a scale of one to ten, how committed are you? What would make it an eight?
  • How was this conversation for you?
